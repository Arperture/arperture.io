Scroll the sheets, then answer the four questions at the bottom. Four answers and we are clear straight through to delivery on the 26th.

Every character is a multi angle sheet

Front, three quarter, and either profile or back. Neutral light, neutral wardrobe, plain grey. Every clip generates against these files rather than against a text description.

The three Chelsis are one person

Twenty one is derived from fourteen, and fourteen from seven. Mary at forty two is aged from Mary at twenty seven the same way. They are not three actors who resemble each other.

The restaurant lot is one place

The playground and the payphone were built against the wide, so the same building, sign, fence and sky sit behind all three. In Scene 1 we cut between them, and if the geography disagreed you would feel it without being able to name it.

The location sheets are deliberately ungraded

Period accurate in design, photographed neutral, with no film treatment applied. That is on purpose, and it connects to decision one. Either answer works without rebuilding anything.

Your call

Four decisions

These are the only things standing between here and generation. Answer them in any form you like, a reply on Upwork is fine.

Decision one

The film stock

You asked for heavy 1990s cinematic film grain across the entire 110 seconds. I do not think that is what you want, and I would rather raise it now than deliver it and have you see the problem in the cut.

A 1980 playground rendered on 1990s stock is the same era slippage we corrected on Thursday. Your own reference photos make the case. The playground and the birthday party are unmistakably late seventies Kodak: warm, magenta shifted, soft, heavy grain, halated highlights, low contrast. Nothing in those frames looks like a 1995 feature.

There is a storytelling cost too. The audience reads fifteen years passing partly through the image itself, and one stock across all six scenes erases that.

What I would build instead is three profiles:

1980 · Scenes 1 and 2

Warm, faded, magenta shifted, coarse grain, heavy halation, milky blacks. Overcast grey sky on warm degraded stock. Your reference photos exactly.

1987 · the overpass

Still film but a later generation. Higher contrast, cleaner blacks, finer grain. Sodium orange against cold night blue.

1994 to 95 · Scenes 3 to 6

Where the heavy 1990s cinematic grain belongs, and where it will look correct. Fine even grain, deep blacks, rich controlled colour.

The effect is that the film gets cleaner and brighter as she rises. Grubby and warm in 1980, hard and cold in 1987, rich and controlled in 1995, blinding white at the crown. Your viewer will not consciously register the stock changing, but he will feel the picture open up across the ninety seconds.

If you want uniform 1990s grain across all six scenes I will build it that way without argument. I just want it to be your decision rather than a side effect.

Need from you: three stock profiles, or uniform 1990s grain throughout.
Decision two

Scene 4, the doorstep

I am proposing this scene look deliberately plain. Neutral, bright, low contrast, almost bland.

It is the only scene in the trailer with no visual tension, because it is the life Chelsi ended up with instead of the one Scene 2 gave her. Grading it as beautifully as the amber scenes would flatten that meaning. It should look comfortable, unremarkable, and slightly airless.

Need from you: confirm the plain treatment, or say the word and it gets the same prestige grade as the rest.
Decision three

Scene 6, the coronation

Not addressed in your notes, so I am proposing it. Blown out theatrical white, hard specular flashbulbs, contrast at its highest but bright rather than dark, and grain reduced to near zero.

After ninety seconds of texture, the sudden cleanliness is the effect. It is the payoff for the whole grade progression.

Need from you: approve, or tell me you want the coronation to carry the same grain as the rest.
Decision four

The opening eight seconds

You told me this is for a private financier watching on a laptop. That changes how I would open.

As the script runs, we open on a child's hands on a rusted playground bar, then a wide of an empty parking lot. On a monitor that is patient and confident. On a laptop, to someone who has already been pitched four times this month, it is eight seconds of nothing happening.

What I would do instead, using shots already in the list and requiring no new work:

0 to 2sA tear on the crowned woman's cheek, flashbulbs firing.
2 to 4sHard cut. A child's hands gripping a rusted playground bar, 1980.
4 to 6sMary crying at the payphone.
6 to 8s"One day, you're gonna be a princess."

The whole film in eight seconds, opening on the most expensive looking image you have and then spending six seconds showing what it cost. It also sets up the two second match cut at the very end, so the trailer opens and closes on the same rhyme.

Need from you: yes or no. Fully reversible up to picture lock on the 24th.
